It's fourth down.
Now what?

A walkthrough of how a dynamic programming model decides when to punt, kick, or go for it — and why NFL coaches still get it wrong.

An interactive companion to a replication of Romer (2003)
Section 01 — The setup

What's the right call?

It's 4th down. Your team needs 3 yards. You're at midfield. What should you do?

Your three options each lead to a different future state of the game:

  • Punt — give the ball to the opponent, but they start far from your end zone.
  • Field goal — try for 3 points, but if you miss, the opponent gets the ball at the spot of the kick.
  • Go for it — try to gain the 3 yards. Succeed and you have a fresh set of downs. Fail and the opponent takes over right where you are.

To choose well, you need to know what each of those future states is worth. That's the foundation of the whole model. Let's build it up step by step.

Section 02 — The value function

What's a yard line worth?

For any spot on the field, the value is the expected net points your team will score, on average, over the rest of the game.

Notice the curve has a few interesting properties:

  • Near your own goal line, the value is negative. Being pinned at your own 1 means you'll likely punt soon, giving the opponent good field position — and they'll often score.
  • Around your own 15, the value crosses zero — the break-even point.
  • The value rises smoothly across the field, accelerating near the opponent's end zone where touchdowns become likely.
Section 03 — Value iteration

How do we compute the values?

The clever part: we don't need to know the values to start. We just guess, then refine.

This is called value iteration. The Bellman equation says:

Value of where I am = points scored before next state + value of where I end up next.

The recursion looks circular — to find one value, we need other values. But it converges if we start with any guess and refine repeatedly. Step through the iterations to see how information propagates backward through the chain.

Section 04 — Component models

Three more pieces we need

To compare options, we need to know the probability of each outcome. Three small models handle this.

How likely is the field goal to be made?

A logistic regression on kick distance. Almost automatic from short range, dropping below 50% past 60 yards.

Modern NFL kickers make about 73% of 50-yard attempts and 49% of 60-yarders.

What is a punt worth?

This one's trickier — punts can be returned, blocked, downed, fair-caught, or even returned for touchdowns. The exported model rolls those outcomes into one expected-value curve.

The expected value of punting is almost always negative (you give the ball away). The question on 4th down is which option is least negative.

How likely is the offense to convert?

Another logistic regression, this time on yards-to-go. Field position barely matters across most of the field — what matters is how far you have to go.

Romer used 3rd-down plays as a proxy for 4th-down outcomes; modern data has enough 4th-down attempts to verify the assumption holds.

What this model can and can't do

This decision engine assumes risk-neutrality over points — a 50% chance of 7 points equals a guaranteed 3.5. That's a fine approximation early in games but breaks down late, when score and time create asymmetric incentives. Real production systems (like nfl4th) use win probability instead.

It also uses league-average rates for everything. A team with a great offense and a weak defense should arguably be more aggressive than the model suggests; a team with the opposite profile should be more conservative.

Despite these limitations, the model robustly finds the same headline result Romer found in 2003: NFL coaches are systematically more conservative than expected-value-maximizing. Twenty years of analytics evangelism have moved the needle, but not nearly enough.
